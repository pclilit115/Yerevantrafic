 1. Executive summary
 Yerevan has a growing registered vehicle fleet, a restructured public transport network and several documented congestion
 corridors. However, publicly accessible sources do not provide a complete, continuously updated dataset of traffic volume
 (vehicles/hour), speed by street segment and time of day, or the number of vehicles physically circulating in the city at a
 given moment.

 The most defensible approach for a realistic visualization is to combine: (1) official fleet and public-transport inventory data;
 (2) reported speed indicators; (3) documented congestion events and corridors; and (4) clearly labelled estimates for any
 missing segment-level values.

 2. Vehicle fleet and public transport
 2.1 Registered vehicle stock
 The municipality reported 343,220 registered vehicles in Yerevan as of 1 September 2025, up by 11,906 from the previous
 year. The national total was 953,965. A later urban-monitoring review repeats the 343,220 figure, but its underlying dated
 reference is still September 2025. Use the dated official figure rather than treating it as a verified November 2026 register
 update.

 An academic article published in 2026 reports that approximately 88% of Yerevan's registered vehicles were light passenger
 vehicles, based on the 1 September 2025 fleet. This supports a passenger-car-dominant model, but does not give the exact
 number of cars active at peak hour.

 The municipal page also records fleet acquisitions during 2020–2024: 100 small GAZelle City buses, 562 Zhong Tong
 medium buses and 87 MAN large buses. Fifteen Yutong and fifteen Zhong Tong trolleybuses were acquired during
 2022–2024. The published total of 752 buses is a stock/inventory figure; the weekday operational figure of 632 is the better
 input for a road-traffic visualization.

 2.3 Passenger activity
 An SDG Spotlight Report for Armenia gives annual Yerevan public-transport passenger totals for 2022: buses 74.1 million;
 minibuses 44.2 million; trolleybuses 4.3975 million; metro 23.317 million; total 146.0165 million. These are annual
 passenger trips/validations, not unique people and not vehicles. A later 2026 monitoring page reports 3.6 million
 public-transport fare validations in the first week of September 2026; treat that as a short-period validation count, not a daily
 average.

 3.3 Time-of-day pattern
 An Armenian government decision text describes the most intensive city traffic as concentrating in morning and evening
 peak periods, each typically lasting about 1.5–2 hours. It does not provide a validated citywide hourly profile. A documented
 severe event occurred between 08:00 and 08:30 on 22 April 2026. For a simulation, 07:30–09:30 and 17:00–19:00 are
 reasonable initial peak windows, but the evening window should be labelled as a modelling assumption until measured
 locally.

 Network structure matters
 The 2020 ADB Initial Environmental Examination notes that the limited number of bridges over the Hrazdan River creates
 bottlenecks and substantial peak-hour congestion. It also refers to illegal/double parking and weak enforcement as
 constraints on mobility. This is useful for modelling bridge approaches, junction queues, curbside friction and lane blockage,
 but it is a historical assessment rather than a current traffic count.

 The same assessment states that, in 2009, traffic exceeded design capacity on more than 20% of the road network, while a
 further 35% was approaching its limit. These figures are old and must not be used as a 2026 congestion map; they only
 indicate that capacity stress was a longstanding structural issue.

 What is not available publicly
 No reliable open dataset was found giving (a) vehicles/hour for each street segment, (b) average speed by
 street and hour for a typical weekday, (c) queue length in metres, (d) daily active vehicles versus registered
 vehicles, or (e) a complete bus-lane GIS layer with current operating hours and direction. Do not fabricate
 these values.

 5. Dedicated public-transport lanes
 The municipality announced a continuous dedicated public-transport lane in July 2025 running along Mashtots Avenue and
 continuing through Khorenatsi, Beirut, Italy and Argishti Streets, connecting public transport toward Victory Bridge and in the
 opposite direction. Traffic arrangements at several intersections were adjusted to accommodate the corridor.

 In October 2024, the municipality also described planned or discussed extensions from Kentron toward Arshakunyats
 Avenue, Garegin Nzhdeh Street and Bagratunyats Street, plus a possible connection linking Nor Nork, Arabkir and
 Malatia-Sebastia. These were plans/discussions at the time of the announcement, not proof that all such lanes were
 subsequently implemented.

 For bus animation, use actual route geometry and stop dwell times. Do not set buses to a constant 20.6 km/h: that figure is
 a 2023 peak-hour average across primary thoroughfares. A route-level model should include acceleration, stopping,
 passenger boarding, intersection delay and any dedicated-lane advantage.

 Suggested request to the municipality
 Request anonymized, aggregated traffic data for a typical weekday and weekend, in 15-minute intervals, by road segment
 and direction, including vehicle counts, average speed, occupancy, queue length, vehicle class, bus GPS travel times,
 traffic-signal plans and the current GIS layer of dedicated public-transport lanes. Ask for the date range, sensor coverage,
 measurement method and missing-data rate.

 Quality-control rules
 1. Keep every observation tied to a timestamp and direction.
 2. Keep registered fleet counts separate from active traffic counts.
 3. Keep bus fleet size separate from buses operating simultaneously.
 4. Mark incident-day congestion separately from a typical weekday.
 5. Record the source and confidence for every number.
 6. Never interpolate across missing streets without labelling the result as estimated.
